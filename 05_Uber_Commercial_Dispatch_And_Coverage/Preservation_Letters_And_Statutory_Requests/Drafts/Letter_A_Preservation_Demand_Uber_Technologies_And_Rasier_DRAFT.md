**The duty to preserve**

Under Texas law a duty to preserve evidence "arises only when a party knows or reasonably should know that there is a substantial chance that a claim will be filed and that evidence in its possession or control will be material and relevant to that claim." Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Johnson, 106 S.W.3d 718, 722 (Tex. 2003); Brookshire Bros., Ltd. v. Aldridge, 438 S.W.3d 9 (Tex. 2014). A substantial chance of litigation exists when "litigation is more than merely an abstract possibility or unwarranted fear," and a party may reasonably anticipate suit "before the plaintiff manifests an intent to sue." Brookshire Bros., 438 S.W.3d at [pin] (quoting National Tank Co. v. Brotherton, 851 S.W.2d 193, 204 (Tex. 1993)). Uber has had notice of this collision at least since claim 26-343820011 was opened and, in the words of Uber's own notice to Ms. Dashora, "information about your recent crash has been shared with Progressive." Both addressees have notice through this letter at the latest. This letter confirms in writing that a claim is anticipated and identifies the evidence that is material to it.

Whether a party has spoliated evidence, and what remedy is proportionate, are questions of law for the trial court in the suit in which the evidence is relevant; the remedies available include those under Texas Rule of Civil Procedure 215.2 and, where the court finds that the spoliating party acted with the specific intent of concealing discoverable evidence and that a less severe remedy would be insufficient to reduce the prejudice (or, in the rare case, that a negligent failure to preserve irreparably deprived the other party of any meaningful ability to present a claim or defense), a spoliation instruction. Brookshire Bros., 438 S.W.3d at [pin]; Trevino v. Ortega, 969 S.W.2d 950 (Tex. 1998). This letter is written so that the question need not arise.

Independently of the common-law duty, Texas Occupations Code § 2402.151 (amended by Acts 2025, 89th Leg., R.S., Ch. 227 (H.B. 4215), effective September 1, 2025, which added subsection (a-1) for delivery network companies and left subsection (a) unchanged) provides, in subsection (a): "A transportation network company shall maintain: (1) records evidencing compliance with the requirements of this chapter for a period of two years; (2) individual ride records for at least five years after the date the ride was provided; and (3) driver records for at least five years after the date the driver ceases to be authorized to log in as a driver on the company's digital network." Uber's own Privacy Notice for drivers and delivery people (last updated September 1, 2026) states that data is retained "for 7 years from collection (unless you first delete your Uber account) if necessary for purposes of Uber's tax, insurance, legal or regulatory requirements; subject to Uber's legitimate interests in defending against, or asserting, legal claims," and its retention table lists Location Data, Trip/Delivery, Communications and Usage Data at 7 years and Device data at the life of the account plus 7 years. Ride records and driver records among the categories below are within § 2402.151(a); most of the remaining categories are location, trip, communications, usage or device data to which the Privacy Notice assigns those periods. None of those retention periods has run for any record dated August 28, 2026.

This letter does not ask for the disclosure of the personal identifying information of the passenger who made the canceled request. Texas Occupations Code § 2402.153(a) permits a transportation network company to disclose a passenger's personal identifying information to a third party only if one of three conditions is met, one of which is that "the disclosure is required by a legal obligation"; whether and how that information is produced will be addressed by legal process. This letter asks only that it be preserved.
